# CRITIQUE v4 — Fable, consequence seat: §5's reversing entries

**Verdict: the instrument is right; §5 ships it with no reference, no bounds, no authorisation, and no period close — as specified, it is the backdoor.** Compensating entries are how double-entry accounting has corrected errors for centuries without editing a line, but only because those four constraints come attached. §5 attaches none.

**Visibility detects; it does not prohibit.** An append-only ledger with unconstrained negative entries reaches every net total a mutable ledger can reach — append-only constrains the representation, not the effect. Proof the defence fails: the forbidden act can be performed *in compliance with it*. Append `-500` against last quarter, then `+600` under this quarter's interpretation. Both originals stand, everything is visible, `INV-LEDGER-APPEND-ONLY` holds — and history has been re-priced, Ruling 6's exact prohibition. A control the attack satisfies is not a control; it is a receipt.

**Nothing in §5 stops the `-500`.** RBAC is specified only negatively — the controller loses `update`/`patch`/`delete`. Who may *append* a reversal is never said. Worse, §5's own crash policy implies it cannot be the metering writer: deterministic IDs `(leaseUID, segmentStart, authorizingVersion)` with idempotent create mean re-emitting a segment no-ops, so reversals need a second ID scheme and a second principal — both unnamed. An unnamed principal with unbounded negative append is a mutable ledger with better marketing.

One real mitigation exists: **"the ledger never gates funding."** The `-500` cannot re-fund the scheduler, so reversed hours cannot be re-spent as capacity; the backdoor is fiscal, not scheduling. But cost is conserved — the watts were burned. A reversal does not delete 500 hours, it detaches them from a tenant; at reconciliation they return as unattributed overhead smeared across every other tenant. Ruling 9 violated in fiscal form: spent quota taken back, in Kubernetes, bill forwarded to the innocent.

**The distinction §5 never draws.** Two things get called correction: (a) the meter lied — it recorded hours never physically consumed (replay double-count, wrong multiplier); (b) the spend was real and someone regrets or re-classes it. Ruling 6 protects what happened, not what a buggy meter wrote about it — so (a) restores protected history. (b) is precisely what Rulings 6 and 9 forbid. §5 gives both the same write path.

**Replacement.** A reversing entry must reference exactly one original by its deterministic ID, be bounded by it (|amount| ≤ original, never freestanding), carry a machine-checked reason code restricted to metering-fault classes, and be writable only by a correction principal the metering writer's role cannot assume. Past a settlement horizon, entries are immutable even to correction; regretted-but-real spend is refused at the API — Ruling 9's recourse is organisational.

**What the tenant sees.** §5 says the audit trail shows both, and stops; "status fields are projections of the ledger" never says projections of raw or net. The answer: **net, on every surface, with each reversal rendered as a line item** — date, amount, reason code, referenced entry — bank-statement style, raw recoverable on the same page. **Chargeback carries net**: billing raw invoices tenants for acknowledged metering bugs. If the dashboard shows raw while the invoice shows net, every correction becomes a dispute and the meter's credibility dies at first disagreement. An issued invoice is itself accrued history under Ruling 6: a late reversal lands as a credit line in the current period, never a restated prior invoice.
